# Disclosure Statement

The author of this analysis is a parent of a student directly impacted by this class size issue. Every effort has been made to present the evidence objectively and avoid emotionally charged arguments; however, bias is inherent to human nature, and interested parties are encouraged to evaluate the evidence on its own merits.

# Context

## The Email

On March 26, 2026, Upper Merion Area School District (UMASD) Superintendent Dr. Tamara Thomas Smith, Ed.D. (Dr. Smith) [sent an email](/sources/2026-03-26---class-size) to Bridgeport Elementary School (BES) families to address concerns about second grade class sizes during the 2025–26 school year. By that point, Kristen Wartman's (Mrs. Wartman) class had reached 28 students — well beyond the 23-student class size guidance UMASD had established for elementary education.

Dr. Smith offered three explanations for this outcome:

- "Predicting enrollment is not an exact science."
- "Each principal receives a designated number of staff based on total building enrollment," and it is the principal's responsibility to make "building-level decisions" regarding that allocation.
- The current staffing plan was a product of former principal Timothy Bickhart (Mr. Bickhart), and incoming principal Dr. Grazel had "inherited" that plan.

Dr. Smith also addressed family requests to remediate the situation, stating that the disruption to students' routines would outweigh the benefit of any mid-year changes. Those corrective arguments are set aside here; this analysis focuses exclusively on the preventive measures that address root causes.

Dr. Smith's implication that responsibility rested primarily with prior administrative decisions drew notable dissatisfaction from several readers. While that framing may have shaped the emotional response of affected families, this analysis does not dwell on it — it focuses on root cause.

## BES PTC Meeting

On May 5, 2026, Dr. Smith presented at a BES PTC meeting dedicated to this issue. All source material from that meeting is available [here](/events/2026-05-28---bes-class-size). The key points raised were:

- Had grade 3 been selected as the grade with one fewer classroom teacher, no class size issue would have arisen.
- The reduction in classroom teacher headcount resulted from reassignment or a decision not to backfill departing staff.
- No contingency plan exists should this situation recur, as it is considered an "anomaly."
- The principal is responsible for allocating headcount within the building.
- When asked directly who holds accountability for headcount decisions at the district level, no clear answer was provided.

# Root Cause Analysis

The author of this analysis is trained in [Root Cause Analysis (RCA)](https://asq.org/quality-resources/root-cause-analysis) — a systematic problem-solving process used to identify the fundamental, underlying causes of an issue or defect. Rather than addressing surface-level symptoms, RCA seeks to understand *why* an event occurred so that permanent, effective solutions can be implemented.

## Initial Problem Statement

A well-formed problem statement is the foundation of any RCA. Both parties to this discussion would likely agree on the following:

Where the parties diverge is on the *why*. To work toward that answer, this analysis begins with one of the most accessible and effective tools in the RCA toolkit: the 5 Whys.

## The 5 Whys

[The 5 Whys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_whys) is a simple yet powerful RCA technique. By repeatedly asking "why?" — typically five times — you can peel away surface-level symptoms to uncover the true origin of a problem.

Based on her public statements, Dr. Smith's implied analysis looks like this:

**5 Whys — Implied by Dr. Smith**

1. **Why did second grade class sizes reach 28 students?**
    - Second grade had higher net enrollment than anticipated.
    - The principal selected the wrong grade for the one-teacher reduction.

</div>

Dr. Smith's statements offer two parallel answers to the first why — but the analysis goes no further. There is no exploration of why the wrong grade was selected, nor why enrollment is characterized as an anomaly. This is precisely the pattern the 5 Whys technique is designed to prevent: stopping at a symptom rather than the cause.

The following sequence takes the analysis further:

<div style={{ background: '#f3f4f6', borderLeft: '4px solid #9ca3af', borderRadius: '6px', padding: '1.25rem 1.5rem', margin: '1.5rem 0' }}>

**Root 5 Whys**

1. **Why did 2nd grade class sizes reach 28 students?**
    - 2nd grade had 56 students enrolled across only two classroom teachers.
2. **Why did 2nd grade have only two classroom teachers?**
    - 2nd grade was selected to receive one fewer classroom teacher relative to all other grades. *(Teacher Allotment — see below)*
    - The district characterizes 2nd grade enrollment as higher than anticipated. *(Classroom Teachers — see below)*

Both sequences agree through this point — but there is more to uncover. Each branch is explored in turn below.

### Classroom Teachers

We know the principal was required to designate one grade to receive a reduced teacher allotment, and that this constraint was imposed by the number of classroom teachers available at the building level. The precise rationale behind Mr. Bickhart's selection of 2nd grade is unknown; however, it is worth noting that [grades 3 through 8 are subject to PSSA testing](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/education/programs-and-services/instruction/elementary-and-secondary-education/assessment-and-accountability/pennsylvania-system-of-school-assessment-pssa). Allocating fewer resources to a non-tested grade in order to protect PSSA-measured performance would be a reasonable administrative approach — but without direct evidence of Mr. Bickhart's reasoning, this branch cannot be evaluated further.

The more tractable question is why a grade had to be selected for a teacher reduction at all. The answer is that Bridgeport had only 14 classroom teachers across five grades.

Although Dr. Smith's responses regarding district-level headcount decisions were ambiguous, it is reasonable to conclude that UMASD, in coordination with the school board, holds final authority over staffing levels. Evidence as to whether Mr. Bickhart formally requested the reduction would help clarify the causal chain, but that information is not available. What the data does show is that at the end of the 2023–24 school year, every UMASD elementary school lost one net classroom teacher — with the exception of Gulph Elementary (GLPH), which followed one year later in 2024–25. This pattern is consistent with a deliberate district-level decision to reduce headcount through attrition without backfill.

The rationale for that decision remains unknown, and no public announcements regarding plans to restore classroom teacher staffing have been made, though that may change following the school board meeting scheduled for June 1st. Several explanations are plausible:

- Declining enrollment
- Budgetary constraints
- Staff redeployment to other roles or buildings

This analysis tests the declining enrollment hypothesis first, as compensation data and district-wide redeployment records are not publicly available.

#### Declining Enrollment

Declining enrollment is the natural first hypothesis to test — it was raised during the PTC meeting, and it is the most straightforward to evaluate using available data. A [Right to Know request](/sources/2026-05-18---rtk) produced enrollment figures for UMASD elementary schools by grade level. Genuine decline would be expected to produce downward trends in raw student counts and elevated year-over-year variability.

<iframe src="/figures/enrollment_dashboard.html?figure=1" style={{ width: '150%', height: '700px', border: 'none' }} />

The data do not support a narrative of declining enrollment. At Bridgeport specifically, Grade 2 has exceeded the 23-student-per-teacher guideline in two consecutive years — 2024–25 and 2025–26 — making it the only elementary school in the district to breach this threshold in the past three years. Across all elementary schools, the percent relative standard deviation (%RSD) for total enrollment is 6.6% or less, indicating that building-level enrollment is highly predictable from year to year.

Figure 2 moves beyond this univariate view by applying Cohort Survival Rate (CSR) — a standard metric in education planning that uses the prior year's grade-level enrollment to project the following year's. A CSR below 1.0 indicates contraction; above 1.0 indicates growth.

<iframe src="/figures/enrollment_dashboard.html?figure=2" style={{ width: '150%', height: '700px', border: 'none' }} />

Figure 2a shows that the vast majority of CSR values across all schools and grade transitions cluster near 1.0, indicating stable enrollment. The notable exceptions are the K→G1 and G1→G2 transitions at Candlebrook. The 2-year projection confirms that nearly every school is flat, with Candlebrook as the sole exception — and its CSR trend points upward, not downward. Figure 2b reinforces this: five-year average CSR by school ranges from 0.999 to 1.056, and no school shows a statistically meaningful trend toward enrollment decline.

With the declining enrollment hypothesis unsupported by the data, and with compensation and redeployment records not publicly available, this branch of the analysis cannot be taken further at this time.

### Teacher Allotment

The argument repeated most often during the PTC meeting was that had the correct grade been shorted one teacher, no issue would have arisen. However, Figure 1 already shows that this is not the first time Grade 2 at Bridgeport has exceeded threshold — which means it cannot reasonably be characterized as an anomaly. For grade selection to be a reliable safeguard, enrollment would need to be predictable enough that the building administration could consistently identify the correct grade to reduce with high confidence, year after year.

To assess that likelihood, it is useful to compare four staffing scenarios against one another and against the 24:1 threshold:

- **Actual Staffing:** the recorded allocation each year.
- **Optimal — Actual Teachers:** the same headcount redistributed to minimize the worst-grade student-to-teacher ratio.
- **Uniform — Max Teachers:** the maximum historical teacher total distributed equally across grades.
- **Optimal — Max Teachers:** the maximum historical teacher total redistributed to minimize the worst-grade ratio.

Figure 3a compares all four scenarios against the 24:1 threshold. Figure 3b shows how much the Optimal — Max Teachers scenario improves upon actual ratios. Figure 3c presents the full detail for each school.

<iframe src="/figures/enrollment_dashboard.html?figure=3" style={{ width: '150%', height: '700px', border: 'none' }} />

In Figure 3a, every instance of a threshold breach belongs to Actual Staffing — several of which are projections. Uniform — Max Teachers performs worse than Optimal — Max Teachers, as expected: uniform distribution is inherently less efficient than optimized allocation.

Figure 3c shows that Optimal — Max Teachers keeps every school's worst-grade ratio between 19.0 and 24.3 across all historical and projected years. Optimal — Actual Teachers ranges from 20.7 to 26.0, while Actual Staffing ranges from 20.7 to 28.0 — the latter representing the current situation in Bridgeport's 2nd grade.

In summary: maintaining headcount with optimized allocation results in only one grade-year combination (Gulph 2021–22 G4) exceeding threshold across all schools and years examined. Optimal allocation with current teachers produces three exceedances. Actual staffing produces seven.

The data make clear that optimizing teacher allotment alone — without restoring headcount — is insufficient to reliably prevent threshold exceedances. Even perfect grade selection (Optimal — Actual Teachers) with current resources still produces three threshold breaches across the schools and years examined, directly refuting Dr. Smith's claim that the issue was an anomaly addressable through proper allotment.

## Revised Problem Statement

The initial problem statement established that second grade class size reached an unacceptable level of 28 students. That statement was not altered by this analysis — the problem itself is not in dispute. What the analysis adds is the cause.

Dr. Smith's implied revised problem statement, based on her public statements, is:

# Conclusion

The evidence does not support the district's characterization of this event as an anomaly caused by unpredictable enrollment and an incorrect grade selection. Enrollment across UMASD elementary schools is stable and predictable, with building-level %RSD of 6.6% or less and five-year average CSR values ranging from 0.999 to 1.056. The conditions that produced this outcome were structural, not exceptional.

Both contributing causes identified in this analysis are addressable. Grade selection is the more tractable of the two, but even perfect selection under current headcount produces three threshold breaches across the schools and years examined. It is therefore a necessary but insufficient safeguard.

Reduced headcount is the more consequential cause. Every UMASD elementary school operating under reduced headcount faces the same structural risk each year the reduction persists. Bridgeport's 2nd grade in 2025–26 is the realized version of a risk that remains latent across the district.

Restoring classroom teacher headcount to pre-2023–24 levels, combined with a data-driven allocation process, is the only scenario demonstrated here to reliably keep all grades within the 24:1 threshold. Until headcount is restored, threshold breaches should be treated not as surprises but as expected outcomes of a known structural condition.
